The mill took its water from a leat that ran a half mile along the contour of
the hill, and the man who kept it walked that half mile every morning whether
or not anything was wrong. He said the point was not to find the blockage but
to know the leat well enough that a blockage would look wrong before it was one.
In forty years he was late to the wheel twice, and both times the trouble was
downstream of everything he had looked at, which he took as instruction rather
than as bad luck. The ledgers he left behind record the weather in four words
or fewer and the sacks in exact numbers, and between those two columns there is
nothing at all, which later readers have found either maddening or admirable
depending on what they wanted from him.
Paper made by hand dries unevenly unless the sheets are stacked and pressed
while still damp, and the pressing is where most of the skill is. Too little and
the fibres never lock; too much and the sheet comes out dense and dead, closer
to card than to paper, and takes ink badly ever afterwards. The apprentices
learned the difference by ruining a great deal of stock, because the only
reliable teacher was the sheet itself two days later, and by then whatever the
hands had done was long finished and could not be recalled precisely. One of the
older women kept a habit of pressing a corner of each batch with her thumb and
saying a number aloud, and nobody was ever able to explain what the numbers
meant, including her.
Bells were cast in pits dug beside the church they were meant for, because a
cast bell is easier to hang than to carry. The founders travelled with moulds
and with a reputation, and the reputation was the more valuable of the two: a
bell that cracks in its first winter is remembered for a century in a place
where very little else changes. The tuning was done afterwards, by turning the
bell on a lathe and taking metal from the inside, and it could only ever go one
way, since nothing can be added back. A founder who took too much left a bell
slightly sharp of what the village had paid for, and a founder who took too
little left one that everybody described, forever, as sounding a bit flat. The
surviving accounts are mostly about money and only occasionally about sound,
and where they do mention sound they use words that no longer mean what they
meant, so the modern ear has to guess.
Winter feeding of bees is a matter of weight rather than of sight. The keeper
lifts the back of the hive an inch and judges by the resistance, which sounds
imprecise and is not, in the hands of somebody who has done it for a few years
on the same hives. Opening a hive in cold weather to see how the stores are
holding costs the colony more than the information is worth, so the whole
practice is arranged around learning as much as possible without looking. The
old manuals are full of instructions for this, and they disagree with each
other, and every one of them insists on its own method with complete confidence.
