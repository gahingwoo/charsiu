The lighthouse stood on a spur of granite that the sea had been working at for
longer than anyone had kept records. Its keeper, a man named Ewan Calder, had
come to the rock in the spring of his thirty-fourth year and had not left it
for more than a fortnight at a time since. He kept a journal, not because he
believed anyone would read it, but because the alternative was to let the days
run together until they were indistinguishable, and he had seen what that did
to men on other stations. Each entry began with the barometer and the wind, and
ended with whatever he had thought about while trimming the wick.
In February the storms came up from the south-west and stayed for a week at a
time. The waves broke over the gallery and the salt got into everything: the
hinges, the flour, the pages of the journal itself, which curled and would not
lie flat afterwards. He learned to write in pencil. Ink ran when the damp got
to it, and there was no season on that rock when the damp did not get to it.
The railway came to the valley in the autumn of 1878, and with it the surveyors,
who walked the line in pairs and argued about gradients within earshot of the
farms. What the valley remembered afterwards was not the argument but the noise:
a sound that arrived before the train did and stayed in the rock for a while
after it had gone. The oldest of the surveyors kept a notebook of soil types and
another of birds, and it is the second one that survives, because his daughter
thought the first was work and the second was him. Neither assumption held up.
He had chosen the route along the eastern slope because of where the herons fed,
and the engineers had agreed with him for reasons of drainage that turned out,
forty years later, to have been the same reason described differently. The line
closed in 1963 and the rails were lifted the following spring, but the cutting
is still there, and in summer it fills with the particular silence of a place
built for a noise that has stopped. Sheep use it as shelter. The stone abutments
of the bridge over the beck are sound enough that the county surveyor, visiting
in 1998, wrote that they would outlast the road that replaced them, which was a
joke at the time and is now simply a description of the situation.
